Econ 238 · Storm risk and adaptation

More People in Harm’s Way,
Fewer Storm Deaths per Person.

Official U.S. data show that deaths from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes have generally fallen over time. The drop looks even larger once we account for population growth—even while more people live in hurricane-prone coastal counties.

≈65% lower

Storm deaths went down. The per-person risk fell even more.

Looking only at raw totals can hide an important denominator: the U.S. population more than doubled over this period.

Average annual deaths from tornadoes, floods, and hurricanes generally declined after the 1970s, though individual disasters can make a decade look much worse.

Source: NOAA/NWS Weather Related Fatality and Injury Statistics. 2020–24 is a partial decade.[1]

Why the denominator matters: raw combined deaths fell from about 262 per year in the 1940s to about 218 in 2020–24, but the population-adjusted rate fell from about 1.85 to 0.65 deaths per million.

At the same time, more people live in hurricane-prone coastal areas.

Exposure matters. A storm can become more costly—or threaten more lives—simply because more people and property are in its path.

The Census Bureau reports that the 185 Atlantic and Gulf coastline counties it identifies as most vulnerable to hurricanes grew from 51.9 million people in 2000 to 60.2 million in 2017—a 16% increase.[3]

What this establishes

More people were living in hurricane-exposed coastline counties by 2017 than in 2000. That means the amount of people and property potentially in harm’s way increased.

Important caution

Population growth is not the same as domestic migration. It also reflects births and international immigration. And moving to a southern state does not automatically mean moving into a hurricane zone or floodplain. IRS county-to-county migration files are a better tool for isolating internal migration.[4]

Economics changes the question from “Is there risk?” to “What is the cheapest way to reduce it?”

Risk depends on both the hazard and our exposure to it. We can try to change the hazard, change where people and property are located, or make exposed places safer.

Illustrative “move to higher ground” calculation

The assignment gives a $600 billion estimate for vulnerable structures. If those structures last about 50 years, roughly 2%—about $12 billion—would naturally be replaced each year. If rebuilding in safer locations adds 15% to the replacement cost:

  • $12B/yrcapital replaced as it depreciates
  • $1.8B/yrextra cost for safer locations
  • ≈0.0055%of a $33T economy

This is an assumption-driven classroom exercise, not a forecast. The $600B figure comes from the assignment’s Nordhaus-based framing.

4

Why disaster losses can rise even when protection improves.

The IPCC’s SREX report emphasizes that long-run increases in disaster losses are strongly tied to rising exposure and vulnerability.

Exposure

More people, homes, businesses, and infrastructure are located where storms can hit. That alone can increase total losses.[5]

Vulnerability

Two communities facing the same storm can have very different outcomes because of building quality, warnings, evacuation, wealth, insurance, and emergency response.

Adaptation

If exposure rises while deaths per person fall, that pattern is consistent with better protection. It does not, by itself, prove which adaptation caused the decline.

5

Heat deaths are a useful warning: adaptation is not automatic.

Heat-related mortality is more volatile than the storm-fatality series, and the 1995 Chicago heat wave shows how much social conditions and infrastructure can matter.

1995: a vulnerability shock

NWS data show a large national heat-death spike in 1995. CDC analysis of the Chicago heat wave found especially high risk among older people and people who were socially isolated, while access to air conditioning and social contact were protective.[6]

The lesson is not that temperature does not matter. It is that the same temperature can produce very different death tolls depending on buildings, energy access, public warnings, and behavior.

Burke et al. measure something different

Burke and coauthors estimate temperature-attributable mortality using statistical relationships between temperature and deaths. That is much broader than NWS counts of deaths directly recorded under weather-event categories.[7]

Do not compare the numbers as if they are the same metric. One is an event-counting system; the other estimates how much overall mortality changes with temperature.
“But wait—does this prove Americans have adapted?”

No. The evidence supports a narrower claim: storm deaths per person have fallen while exposure in hurricane-prone coastal counties has grown. That is consistent with adaptation, but other changes—storm mix, reporting, demographics, where growth happened within counties, and chance—can also affect the numbers. Good economics means noticing both the pattern and what the pattern cannot prove.
