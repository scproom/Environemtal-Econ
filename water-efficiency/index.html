The water-efficiency paradox · A 3-minute exhibit

Can saving water
use more water?

A farm can use less water per crop while leaving no extra water for its neighbors. Follow 100 units to see why.

01 / Follow the water

Same basin. Better irrigation. Different outcomes.

Withdrawal is water taken from a river or aquifer. Consumption is water leaving the usable local supply through evaporation and plant transpiration. Return flow is water that runs off or drains back. If it is recoverable, another user can use it. [4][6]

FIRST / HOLD BEHAVIOR FIXED

The appealing promise

At 60% efficiency, supplying 60 crop-water units takes 100 withdrawals. At 80%, it takes 75. If we assume all 25 saved units become additional usable supply, the saving is 25.

THEN / COUNT RETURN FLOWS

The missing water

If those 25 units previously returned to the river or aquifer, modernization removes that supply. Others still have 40: now 25 unwithdrawn + 15 returned. [2][6]

FINALLY / LET FARMERS RESPOND

The incentive to grow

Keeping access to 100 withdrawals lets farmers expand acreage or choose thirstier crops. Consumption can rise from 60 to 80: a 33% increase. This is a possible response, not a forecast. [1][4]

03 / Does this happen outside the model?

The paradox has evidence—and limits.

A broad review found both patterns

80.1%

of cases with withdrawal data reported lower withdrawals (n = 176).

83.2%

of cases with consumption data reported higher consumption (n = 161).

Pérez-Blanco et al. (2020), empirical review: 230 case studies from 213 papers, including modeled and observed outcomes. Different denominators; these percentages do not describe identical groups or predict any one farm. [4]

Technology changes choices

In western Kansas, Pfeiffer & Lin found that adoption of more efficient dropped-nozzle irrigation increased groundwater extraction on average, partly through changed crop patterns. [3]

Efficiency can still help

Reducing unnecessary evaporation or nonrecoverable drainage can save usable water. Technology also supports productivity. Conservation policy must track depletion and constrain total use; a withdrawal cap alone may leave consumption unchanged. [4][5][6]

The next question: whose water is “saved”?

Before subsidizing irrigation, ask: Where do return flows go, how quickly do they return, and who already relies on them? Then test whether limits on consumption—or changes in acreage and crops—would protect that supply.

Model, assumptions & reproducible calculations

This is a water-accounting demonstration, not a fitted hydrological or economic model. Numerical choices are illustrative; the mechanisms follow sources [1], [2], [4] and [6]. The selected basin includes the source, farm and users of reusable returns. The time period is long enough to include flows classified as recoverable. Storage changes and inflows are held fixed.

Old withdrawals W₀ = 100; old crop consumption C₀ = 60; old returns R₀ = 40. For simplicity, all modeled consumption is crop consumption; nonbeneficial evaporation is not modeled separately. The efficiency measure is C/W, not a universal definition of irrigation performance. All nonconsumed withdrawals become returns.

Let e be new efficiency, r the recoverable share of return flows, and b the share of saved withdrawal capacity reused. The same r applies before and after. All inputs are fractions, e.g. 80% = 0.80.

Fixed-production withdrawals Wfixed = 60 / e
New withdrawals W₁ = Wfixed + b × (100 − Wfixed)
New consumption C₁ = e × W₁
New returns R₁ = W₁ − C₁
Withdrawal saving = 100 − W₁
Net basin saving = (100 − W₁) + r × (R₁ − 40)
Equivalent: (60 + (1 − r) × 40) − (C₁ + (1 − r) × R₁)

Why the sign matters: basin saving is old depletion minus new depletion. It counts both reduced consumption and reduced nonrecoverable flows. A negative value means less usable water for others. “Available” does not mean legally transferred, equally valuable, or environmentally surplus. Reallocating a saving to another consuming user need not reduce total basin depletion.
