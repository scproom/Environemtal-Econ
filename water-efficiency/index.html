A better watering system can have a surprising result

Can saving water
use more water?

Follow 100 buckets to see how a farmer can take less water without leaving more for everyone else.

01 / Follow the water

Taking less water does not always mean saving water.

Farmers take water from rivers or underground to grow crops. Some of it turns into water vapor and goes into the air. Some flows back into rivers or underground, where another person may use it again. That water flowing back is the key to this story. [4][6]

1 / GROW THE SAME CROPS

It looks like a saving

The old system takes 100 buckets to give the crops the 60 they need. The better system takes only 75. At first, it looks as though 25 extra buckets are now available to other people.

2 / FOLLOW THE WATER BACK

Those buckets had another job

Before, 40 buckets flowed back for others to use. Now only 15 do. Add the 25 left in place, and others still get 40. The farm takes less, but everyone else gets no extra water. [2][6]

3 / LET THE FARMER GROW MORE

A reason to plant more

If the farmer can still take 100 buckets, the better system can support more crops—or crops that need more water. Then 80 buckets may be used up instead of 60, leaving only 20 for others. This can happen; it is not inevitable. [1][4]

03 / What happens on real farms?

Better systems can lead to more water use.

Researchers found a surprising pattern

80.1%

of the 176 examples with this information showed less water taken.

83.2%

of the 161 examples with this information showed more water used up.

Pérez-Blanco and colleagues (2020) reviewed 230 examples from 213 research papers. Some used real observations; others used computer models. The two percentages cover different groups of examples. They do not tell us what will happen on every farm. [4]

Farmers may change what they grow

In western Kansas, Pfeiffer & Lin studied farmers who switched to better sprinklers. On average, farmers pumped more underground water afterward, partly because they changed the crops they grew. [3]

Better equipment can still help

Better equipment can help grow more food. It can also save water if it cuts evaporation or stops water draining somewhere it cannot be reused. To leave more water for everyone, rules need to limit how much is actually used up, not just how much farms take. [4][5][6]

The next question: whose water is “saved”?

Before paying farmers to upgrade their watering systems, ask: Where does the water flowing back go, how long does it take, and who already uses it? Then ask how growing more crops would change the answer.

04 / Sources and the numbers

Sources

How the numbers work

This is a made-up example to explain an idea, not a prediction for a real farm. Each bucket stands for 10 liters. We follow the river or underground water, the farm, and the people who can reuse water flowing back. We give that water enough time to return.

Start here: the old system takes 100 buckets. Crops use up 60, and 40 flow back. For simplicity, this example counts only water used up by crops; it does not separately calculate evaporation from bare soil. The river’s incoming water and stored water stay the same.

Three settings change the result: how much water the crops use, how much water flowing back can be reused, and how much of the saving the farmer uses to grow more. The share that can be reused stays the same before and after.

Water needed for the same crops = 60 ÷ crop share
Water taken now = water needed + share of saving reused × (100 − water needed)
Water used up now = crop share × water taken now
Water flowing back now = water taken now − water used up now
Extra water for others = (100 − water taken now) + reusable share × (water flowing back now − 40)

Use percentages as decimals in the formulas: 80% = 0.80. A positive result means more water for others; a minus sign means less. Water available to others is water left in place plus water flowing back that they can reuse. This does not automatically give anyone the legal right to that water. If someone else uses up a saving, the region may still use up just as much water overall.

The seven sources
  1. Grafton et al. (2018). “The paradox of irrigation efficiency.” Science 361: 748–750. Explains the main idea and what it means for water policy.
  2. Ward & Pulido-Velázquez (2008). “Water conservation in irrigation can increase water use.” PNAS 105: 18215–18220. Shows how farm choices and water flowing back affect the whole region.
  3. Pfeiffer & Lin (2014). “Does efficient irrigation technology lead to reduced groundwater extraction? Empirical evidence.” JEEM 67: 189–208. Author institution’s abstract. Studies farmers in Kansas.
  4. Pérez-Blanco, Hrast-Essenfelder & Perry (2020). “Irrigation Technology and Water Conservation: A Review of the Theory and Evidence.” REEP 14: 216–239. Reviews the research; supplies the percentages shown above.
  5. Perry & Steduto (2017). “Does Improved Irrigation Technology Save Water? A Review of the Evidence.” FAO. Explains why water rules matter.
  6. FAO (2021). “Guidance on Realizing Real Water Savings with Crop Water Productivity Interventions.” Water Reports 46. Report page. Explains why taking less water is not always a real saving.
  7. Playán et al. (2024). “Irrigation modernization in the Ebro–Aragón region of Spain: Past and future trends.” Agricultural Water Management, article 108975. Describes changes in farming and watering systems in Spain. Background reading, not proof of cause and effect here.
