Econ 238 · Let's Make a Deal · Ballot Initiative

Four dams for the salmon. Would you take the deal?

Breaching four dams on the lower Snake River would give up clean hydropower and cheap barge shipping. In return, Idaho's salmon would get back the river that once carried millions of them. This page lays out the whole bargain, including the parts its supporters would rather skip. Then you vote.

The Proposition

Breach Four Snake River Dams and Pay to Replace Their Benefits

The Ballot Question

Shall Congress direct the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to breach Ice Harbor, Lower Monumental, Little Goose and Lower Granite dams on the lower Snake River by December 31, 2032, and appropriate up to $20 billion over 15 years to first secure replacement carbon-free electricity, upgrade rail and highways for grain shippers, extend irrigation intakes, and compensate affected ports and communities?

If YES wins, you could check that it happened:

  • Before any breaching starts, the Bonneville Power Administration has signed contracts for at least 1,000 average megawatts of new carbon-free power.
  • By December 31, 2032, the earthen section beside each of the four dams is removed, and the Snake runs free for about 140 miles from Lewiston, Idaho to Pasco, Washington.
  • Rail and highway upgrades for grain shippers and new irrigation intakes are funded and under construction before the reservoirs are drawn down.
  • The full amount spent, up to $20 billion, is reported each year to Congress.

What's the Deal?

Fish and a free river, in exchange for power and barges

We buy back about 140 miles of free-flowing river. That is the best remaining chance to recover Snake River salmon and steelhead, which once returned by the millions and are now listed under the Endangered Species Act. In exchange we give up about 1,000 average megawatts of carbon-free hydropower (roughly 5% of the Northwest's electricity, about enough for Seattle). We also lose cheap barge shipping for about 3.8 million tons of cargo a year, mostly wheat, and still water for nearby irrigation. Winners: Columbia Basin tribes, fishers, coastal towns and the Southern Resident orcas that eat Chinook. Losers: the Port of Lewiston, wheat growers, irrigators and Northwest power customers. Federal taxpayers pay. The Murray and Inslee report priced the full replacement at $10.3 to $27.2 billion. We fund about $20 billion, roughly $60 per American.

The Best Case for YES

Dams can be replaced. Extinct salmon cannot.

Everything the dams provide (power, shipping, irrigation) has a known substitute with a known price. Wind, solar, batteries and transmission can replace the electricity. Rail and trucks can carry the grain. Pumps can reach a lower river. Wild salmon have no substitute. For three decades, agencies and courts have spent billions on fish ladders, spill, trucking and barging young fish, and hatcheries, and most Snake River runs are still listed. Removing dams works. After the Klamath dams came down in 2024, Chinook reached Oregon tributaries within months, and more than 20,000 returned the next year. This deal also honors fishing rights the United States promised tribes in 1855 treaties. Instead of fighting in court forever, we pay the people who lose, in full and up front, and end a 30 year stalemate.

The Best Case for NO

A sure clean-energy loss for an uncertain fish gain.

The dams produce clean, steady power that can ramp up during winter cold snaps and summer heat waves, exactly when the grid is most stressed. Replacing it takes 2,300 to 4,300 megawatts of new resources, and every new wind farm and transmission line faces its own permitting fight. If replacement lags, natural gas fills the gap and emissions rise. Barges are also the lowest-carbon way to move grain; shifting to trucks and rail means more diesel and more road wear. Meanwhile, Snake River salmon still have to pass four more dams on the Columbia and survive warming rivers and ocean heat waves. Breaching four may not be enough. Twenty billion dollars could protect far more habitat, or buy far larger carbon cuts, somewhere else.

Where Is the Body Buried?

Nobody can promise how many fish come back

The weakest number is the biological payoff. Ocean conditions and warmer water may matter as much as the dams, and no study can say how many salmon return. The cost range is also huge ($10 to $27 billion), and the high end could grow. Real people get hurt: Lewiston, Idaho stops being a seaport, and family wheat farms pay more to ship. And the inconvenient fact: for a few years, breaching probably raises carbon emissions while replacement power is built.

Why Isn't This Already Happening?

Concentrated costs, scattered benefits

Shippers, irrigators, ports and public utilities in eastern Washington and Idaho know exactly what they would lose, and they are organized. The winners (fishers, tribes, coastal towns, orca lovers, future generations) are spread out. The federal government owns the dams, and the Army Corps cannot breach them without an act of Congress, where the region's own representatives mostly say no. Policy flips with each president: the Biden administration's 2023 salmon agreement with tribes was revoked by President Trump in June 2025. In 2021, Idaho Republican Mike Simpson proposed a $33.5 billion breach and compensate plan, and it was left out of the infrastructure bill. Uncertainty about fish numbers lets each side cite its own studies. Status quo bias makes keeping something that exists feel safer than removing it, even though the status quo also costs billions.
