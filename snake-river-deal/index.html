01 / THE PROPOSITION

Breach Four Snake River Dams and Pay to Replace Their Benefits

02 / The ballot question

Shall Congress direct the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to breach Ice Harbor, Lower Monumental, Little Goose and Lower Granite dams on the lower Snake River by December 31, 2032, and appropriate up to $20 billion over 15 years to first secure replacement carbon-free electricity, upgrade rail and highways for grain shippers, extend irrigation intakes, and compensate affected ports and communities?

This is the exact question on the live ballot. The date and funding cap are proposed terms, not established engineering estimates.

03 / WHAT’S THE DEAL?

What are we buying—and giving up?

Trade four lower Snake River dams for a better chance of recovering wild salmon and steelhead. Federal taxpayers would fund up to $20 billion over 15 years for breaching and replacement services. Tribes, fishers and people who value living salmon populations gain; electricity customers, grain shippers, irrigators and port communities face disruption. The 2022 final benefit-replacement report describes roughly 900 average megawatts of annual generation and 3,033 megawatts of installed capacity. Those are different services: replacing annual energy does not guarantee power during a cold snap. Its $10.3–$31.3 billion replacement-and-mitigation range is a 2022-dollar, largely present-value comparison, not a current construction bid. Our $20 billion appropriation is a policy choice, not proof that every cost fits. The bargain puts replacement services before breaching; it cannot promise a particular number of returning fish.

04 / The best case for YES

Buy a benefit the market misses.

Salmon recovery produces benefits that electricity and shipping prices leave out: ecological resilience, recreational fishing, cultural continuity and the value people place on a species surviving. NOAA’s 2022 rebuilding report identifies breaching as part of a wider recovery strategy, alongside habitat and other changes. Existing engineering can remove earthen embankments, modify irrigation intakes and expand transport infrastructure. Existing clean-energy technologies can contribute replacement power, although the complete reliable portfolio needs verification. Federal funding could spread transition costs beyond the communities that bear them. This is a case for purchasing a public environmental benefit and addressing concentrated losses. It is not a claim that every resident wins. Tribal treaty interests also deserve consideration beyond a simple dollar tally. Keeping the dams has ongoing maintenance and ecological costs; doing nothing is a choice with consequences.

05 / The best case for NO

Promises do not keep the lights on.

The most serious objection is delivery risk. A $20 billion cap and a 2032 deadline may not buy replacement electricity, grid reliability, shipping, irrigation and compensation together. The final 2022 report’s upper estimate already exceeds that cap and excludes some costs; inflation and permitting add uncertainty. Wind and solar output cannot be equated with dispatchable hydroelectric capacity. If replacements arrive late, either breaching slips or consumers face higher costs and possible fossil generation. Salmon still encounter other dams, predators and a changing ocean, so the ecological gain is uncertain. Meanwhile, replacement power, rail and roads consume land, materials and public money that could fund other environmental improvements. Rejecting this package need not mean rejecting salmon recovery: it can mean demanding a more credible budget, sequence and comparison with alternatives.

06 / WHERE IS THE BODY BURIED?

A budget is not a guarantee.

The weak link is the promise that compensation and replacements fit inside the budget on schedule. We have no engineering procurement plan demonstrating that. Money may replace shipping equipment without replacing a port community’s identity or every lost livelihood. And breaching is difficult to reverse, while salmon recovery remains uncertain. The $20 billion limit is not a guarantee that affected people are made whole. If replacements cannot be secured first, the proposed timetable fails; silently weakening that condition would change the deal people voted on.

07 / WHY ISN’T THIS ALREADY HAPPENING?

A bargain needs someone who can deliver it.

The benefits and costs land on different people, and no single buyer can assemble the bargain cheaply. Salmon’s public benefits encourage free riding; port users and utilities have direct incentives to organize against concentrated losses. Tribes are sovereign rights holders, not simply another interest group. Congress controls the federal authorization and funding, while agencies, utilities and state permitting bodies control different implementation steps. Promised future compensation is less persuasive than an existing job or power contract. Uncertain costs create incentives to bargain for more protection, and future governments may reverse commitments. Washington Commerce reports that federal withdrawal from the 2023 agreement interrupted replacement-energy analysis in 2025; a new state analysis is being procured in 2026. These are coordination and commitment problems, not evidence that opponents dislike nature. Technical feasibility alone does not establish a welfare gain or political feasibility.

Optional economics note

Compensation is not free.

A payment can redistribute who bears a loss. It does not erase the real resources used to replace power and transport, and public funding has an opportunity cost. A positive total benefit would not automatically make this a Pareto improvement: actual losers would need to be made whole. This proposal has not established that result.

What would count as implementation?
  • Congress authorizes the four named breaches and an appropriation capped at $20 billion over 15 years.
  • Replacement carbon-free electricity, transport and irrigation arrangements are secured before breaching, with compensation arrangements for affected communities.
  • The Corps breaches the four dams by December 31, 2032.

If the budget or sequence cannot meet that date, the package has failed to meet its stated terms. Contracts for annual energy alone would not demonstrate reliable replacement service.

Assume a hypothetical $1 billion annual benefit for 50 years, starting next year. This is a teaching example, not an estimate of salmon benefits.

PV = annual benefit × [1 − (1 + r)−50] / r. At zero, PV is $50 billion. Larger discount rates give less weight to benefits received later. An actual comparison must discount costs on the same basis.

The evidence desk

Check the claims.

  1. Final benefit-replacement report · August 2022

    Executive summary pp. 1, 9–12: 900 aMW, 3,033 MW and $10.3–$31.3 billion. Values generally in April/May 2022 dollars; present values use 50 years and 2.25%. The range is not exhaustive and omits the highest energy scenarios. This replaces the draft figure used in the earlier website.

  2. NOAA · Rebuilding salmon and steelhead

    September 2022 scientific recommendation: a suite of actions including breaching, habitat restoration, hatchery and harvest reform. A recovery strategy, not a guaranteed fish count.

  3. BPA / E3 · Power replacement study

    2022 scenario analysis of replacement resources and costs. Distinguish energy, firm capacity and grid services; assumptions about future technologies materially affect results.

  4. Washington Commerce · Replacement study status

    Accessed October 14, 2026: describes the 2025 interruption and 2026 procurement. Replacement analysis is still an implementation issue, not a completed construction plan.

  5. U.S. Army Corps · Lower Snake River dams

    Owner/operator account of the four multipurpose dams and their navigation, hydropower and other functions. Whole Columbia–Snake shipping totals should not be mislabeled as lower-Snake-only cargo.

Research checked October 14, 2026. No numerical fish recovery forecast or benefit–cost ratio is claimed. Sources describe different assumptions; their estimates should not be added together.
